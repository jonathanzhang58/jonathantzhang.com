On the Evaluation of AI Colonoscopy Tools

Jonathan Zhang and Michael Oberst · October 2026

AI colonoscopy tools such as Medtronic's GI Genius draw boxes around suspected polyps so physicians are less likely to miss them. To be cleared by the FDA, companies must show these tools do not lead to excess unnecessary excisions. The accepted way to show it is a non-inferiority test on the true histology rate (THR), the fraction of excised polyps that turn out to be precancerous. Because THR is a proportion, putting a non-inferiority margin on it can make the test impossible to fail. When the AI arm excises only modestly more polyps than control, the trial passes even if every one of the additional excisions is benign. This is the situation in many real examples, such as the SKOUT trial, where, in expectation, the test would have passed regardless of the pathology of the additional excised polyps.

Colonoscopies and polyps

According to the Mayo Clinic, "a colonoscopy is a procedure used to examine the inside of the colon, rectum, and the end of the small intestine." [1] It is performed with a colonoscope: a thin, flexible tube with a camera at the end that is threaded through the colon and gives the physician a live video feed from inside the organ. The video feed lets the physician spot colon polyps, mounds of tissue that can be precancerous.

The colonoscopy is quite a difficult procedure. The colon is dark and full of twists, turns, and folds that hide polyps. Furthermore, when a physician notices a polyp, they have to decide whether to remove it. Each excision raises the chance of immediate or delayed bleeding, bowel perforation, and the general complications that come with more time under sedation. So if a physician sees a polyp they believe is benign, they will leave it in place. However, the only way to confirm whether a polyp is precancerous is to remove it and send it to pathology.

A recent wave of AI colonoscopy products aims to solve some of these issues. Medtronic's GI Genius Intelligent Endoscopy Module, for example, draws highlighted boxes around suspected polyps in the video feed so the physician is less likely to miss them, with the end goal of lowering colon cancer rates [3]. Competing products have been released by Iterative Health, Odin Medical, MAGENTIQ, Wision AI, and others [4, 5, 6, 7]. The goal of these devices is to reduce the number of cancerous polyps left behind in the body without excessively increasing the number of non-cancerous polyps excised. I.e., reducing the number of false negatives without increasing the number of false positives.

FDA Requirements and Challenges

Like any medical device, these systems must be cleared by the FDA before they can be sold [9]. During that review, the FDA identified several risks that this kind of software poses to patients, including "algorithm failure leading to false positives, resulting in unnecessary patient treatment" and "false negatives, resulting in delayed patient treatments." [10] To be cleared, a company must show that these risks are mitigated [9, 10].

However, several challenges prevent a straightforward analysis. First, as in any binary classification problem, there is a tradeoff between the two error types. If we wanted to make absolutely sure no cancerous polyp was left behind, we could remove everything we see. This gives us perfect recall/TPR but maximum FPR. Conversely, if we were terrified of unnecessary excision, we could excise literally nothing, resulting in perfect precision/TNR but maximum FNR. Obviously, we want to lie somewhere between these two extremes. Further, the false negative count is unidentifiable. By definition, a false negative is a cancerous polyp left in the body. Obviously, if the physician noticed the cancerous polyp, they would have tried to excise it, so this value cannot be directly measured. Finally, as previously mentioned, only the malignancy of excised polyps can be determined. If a physician leaves a polyp in the body because they think it's benign, we cannot know whether or not it was a true negative or a false negative. This is known as a selective labels problem.

Industry Precedent

To satisfy the FDA, most companies run an RCT in which they record the number of colonoscopies performed, the number of polyps excised, and the number of excised polyps with clinically significant histology (i.e. confirmed precancerous on biopsy). From these they compute three statistics and compare them between arms:

$$ \begin{aligned} \text{APC} &= \frac{\text{total adenomas detected}}{\text{total colonoscopies}} \\[6pt] \text{ADR} &= \frac{\text{colonoscopies with at least one adenoma detected}}{\text{total colonoscopies}} \\[6pt] \text{THR} &= \frac{\text{excisions with confirmed significant histology}}{\text{total excisions}} \end{aligned} $$

APC is adenomas per colonoscopy, ADR is the adenoma detection rate, and THR is the true histology rate, sometimes reported as the positive percent agreement (PPA) [4, 11]. THR is simply the precision, or positive predictive value, of the physicians' excision decisions.

This was the approach Medtronic used for GI Genius, the first device of this kind to reach the market [3]. Because the FDA accepted it as sufficient and approved the device, most competitors perform the same study to gain FDA approval [4, 5, 10].

However, companies are not required to show that any of these statistics improve. Instead, they only need to demonstrate non-inferiority. Under the FDA's statistical guidance, non-inferiority is shown by testing that a statistic does not fall from the control arm to the AI arm by more than some "clinically relevant" margin [12]. The GI Genius trial set the precedent: margins of 10 percentage points for ADR, 0 for APC (superiority test), and 15 percentage points for THR [3].

This method is reasonable for ADR and APC. However, we will demonstrate that it has strange implications when testing for THR, resulting in current evaluation methods failing to properly determine whether or not these systems truly do not cause excessive unnecessary excisions.

Skill versus threshold from first principles

Let's ignore current methods for now and refer to first principles. As previously stated, we are interested in determining if these systems excessively increase false positives. This goal stems from the concern that such systems do not actually make physicians better at performing colonoscopies. Instead, by highlighting polyps that physicians would have already seen, it encourages physicians to excise things that they would have previously not. Thus, the only difference is that the physicians adopted a more liberal excision policy. So, is it then possible in any way to design a test that determines whether or not physicians in the AI-assisted arm are truly better, or simply more liberal?

The colonoscopy problem can almost be framed as a binary classification problem in machine learning. We have two classifiers: the unassisted physician and the physician with AI assistance. Can we determine if these classifiers are distinct, or if they have the same underlying classification function? One tool we have is the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. Typically, the area under this curve is used as a metric for the effectiveness of your classifier. This curve plots the true positive rate (TPR) against the false positive rate (FPR), parameterized by the classification threshold. As we marginally decrease the threshold, because our underlying classification function has not changed at all, the number of true positives and false positives must increase. Thus, this curve is monotonically increasing.

In our trial, we can identify the false positive and true positive rates of the physicians in each arm of our trial. This yields two separate points within the FPR-TPR axes. If these points were drawn from separate curves, one of which has greater area under the curve, then we can say that AI-assistance changes physician skill. However, if they are drawn from the same curve, then AI-assistance only changes the excision policy of physicians. Can we determine this from the identity of the two points alone?

In general, this problem is impossible. In the figure above, for many selections of two points, despite them coming from possibly different curves, there exists a single curve that parameterizes both. However, if one point lies up and to the left of the other, by the monotonicity of the ROC curve, it is impossible to draw a curve that goes through both, and thus they must be from different curves.

Thus, one way that we can test for whether or not a treatment changes physician skill is to examine the slope between the false positive rate and true positive rates of our two arms. If that slope is negative, which can be determined by a hypothesis test, it must be true that there's a skill difference between the two arms.

$$ \Delta_{AI} = \frac{\Delta FP}{\Delta TP} = \frac{FP_1 - FP_0}{TP_1 - TP_0}, $$

where subscript 0 is the control arm and 1 is the AI arm. (Note: although we only observe counts, not rates, but both rates share the same denominator so they cancel.)

When this test rejects in expectation, we can be sure that the skill between the two arms is different. However, as we discussed prior, two points can come from different ROC curves but still have non-negative values of \(\Delta_{AI}\). Thus its power is very weak. In fact, for any non-negative value of \(\Delta_{AI}\), one can construct a classifier that produces exactly those counts through a threshold change alone. Consider the following

The AI helped physicians excise 15 additional malignant polyps with no change in benign excisions. That looks like strong evidence of improved skill. Yet \(\Delta_{AI} = 0 / 15 = 0\), so the test does not reject.

How can fifteen extra cancers caught with zero extra benign excisions be explained by a threshold shift? Consider the plot of the physician's estimated malignancy probability \(\hat\pi(X)\) against the true probability \(P(M = 1 \mid X)\). We have placed no restrictions on the physician's estimate, so we are free to draw a curve such as:

Polyps to the right of the vertical line are malignant in expectation; polyps to the left are benign. The horizontal line is the excision threshold \(\zeta\); everything above it is excised. The curve therefore splits into four regions matching the four cells of the confusion matrix. This physician is almost antagonistic. the polyps most likely to be malignant are assigned a lower estimate than the polyps least likely to be malignant. Starting at \(\zeta = 1/2\), lowering the threshold to just under \(9/20\) excises a block of polyps that are all malignant in expectation, raising true positives with no new false positives. Thus a threshold change reproduces the table above.

Clearly, this example is contrived. Real physicians are not adversarial. So let's add the following assumption: the physician's estimate is monotonically non-decreasing in the true probability. If one polyp is genuinely more likely to be malignant than another, the physician does not think it is less likely. This is fairly mild. A physician who systematically ranked dangerous polyps as safer than safe ones would not be a physician for long.

Under this assumption, if there is no change in skill between arms (\(H_0\)), it can be shown that \(\Delta_{AI}\) must fall in the interval:

$$ \frac{FP_0}{TP_0} \;\le\; \Delta_{AI} \;\le\; \frac{TN_1}{FN_1}. $$

The true negative and false negative counts on the right are unidentifiable, so we cannot use the upper bound. But the lower bound is made of observable counts, and since counts are non-negative it is at least zero. So we test whether \(\Delta_{AI} < FP_0 / TP_0\). This is strictly stronger than testing \(\Delta_{AI} < 0\). In the example above, \(FP_0 / TP_0 = 90/60 = 1.5 > 0 = \Delta_{AI}\), and we conclude that the AI improved skill, which matches our intuition.

A little algebra shows that this test is exactly equivalent to testing whether THR increases from the control arm to the AI arm. Thus, a superiority test on THR is, under monotonicity, a test of skill.

Non-inferiority Margins

However, trials in practice run a non-inferiority test with a margin \(N\). Thus the null hypothesis is

$$ \text{THR}_0 \;\ge\; \text{THR}_1 + N. $$

Let \(S_0\) and \(S_1\) be the total excisions in each arm, let \(\Delta S = S_1 - S_0\) be the additional excisions attributable to AI assistance, and let

$$ \pi_\Delta = \frac{\Delta TP}{\Delta S} $$

be the malignancy rate among those additional excisions.

Let's assume \(\Delta S > 0\) and \(\Delta TP > 0\). Clearly, if our intervention reduces the number of true positives, it is harmful to patients and should be discarded. Furthermore, if the true positives increase and the number of total exclusions decreases, then the system is clearly beneficial, and further analysis is not required. Furthermore, both in practice and in theory, AI highlighting software should increase excisions and true positives. Thus, this is the only case in which we are concerned.

It can be shown that the test above is equivalent to the following:

$$ \pi_\Delta \;\le\; \text{THR}_0 - N\,\frac{S_1}{\Delta S}. $$

The left-hand side is a proportion, so it is at least 0. Thus, whenever

$$ N\,\frac{S_1}{\Delta S} \;\ge\; \text{THR}_0, $$

the right-hand side is non-positive, and the null is rejected for every possible \(\pi_\Delta\). The test cannot fail, no matter how many of the additional excisions are benign. Rearranging, this happens whenever

$$ \frac{\Delta S}{S_0} \;\le\; \frac{N}{\text{THR}_0 - N}. $$

Consider the following example. The control arm excises 100 polyps and all of them turn out malignant, so \(\text{THR}_0 = 100\%\). The AI arm excises 110. Even if all ten additional polyps are benign, THR only falls to \(100/110 \approx 91\%\), meaning it can never go below \(15\%\). The device passes regardless all of the time, and the test is vacuous.

This is not merely a theoretical observation. These issues come up in practice. In the SKOUT trial, 1,136 polyps were excised in the AI arm and 963 in the control arm [4], so \(S_1 / \Delta S = 1136 / 173 \approx 6.57\). The trial used an absolute margin of \(N = 0.14\) on THR, which permits the additional excisions to have a malignancy rate up to \(0.14 \times 6.57 \approx 0.92\) below the control THR of 0.717. Since \(\pi_\Delta\) is a proportion bounded between 0 and 1, the null hypothesis was guaranteed to be rejected regardless of how many of the additional excisions were malignant. The test was meaningless.

Taken together, the accepted method for demonstrating to the FDA that these products do not harm patients through excess false positives is, in expectation, guaranteed to pass. There is effectively no mitigation of false-positive risk at all.

What to do instead

Clearly, the current burden of proof is not sufficient to demonstrate that these devices prevent excessive false positive excisions. What other options exist to truly demonstrate that these devices help patients?

In a perfect world we could definitively answer this using a longitudinal study: one group of patients receives standard colonoscopies, another receives AI-assisted ones, and we follow both groups for decades and compare colon cancer rates. Unfortunately in practice, this is obviously infeasible due to time and money constraints.

One alternative is a utility-based approach, which has been proposed in certain fields such as criminal justice [13]. In this approach, we assign some kind of quantitative benefit to each additional malignant polyp caught and a quantitative cost to each additional benign polyp unnecessarily removed. These numbers can be determined by asking physicians. However, this only accounts for the excised polyps. During a colonoscopy, the biggest risk is that malignant polyps get left behind in the body. But as we discussed, the counts for this quantity are unidentifiable.

However, although the individual counts are not identifiable, under the randomization of the trial, it can be shown that the change in the number of false negatives and true negatives can be identified. This allows us to identify the change in counts for each quadrant of the confusion matrix. We can then assign the utility weights to each quadrant and calculate the sum. This sum is equivalent to the change in utility between the two trial arms. A simple hypothesis test can be used to determine if using these AI systems results in a superior or at least noninferior total utility.

However, if we want to avoid possibly arbitrary utility weights, another alternative would be to simply keep the THR test, but test for superiority instead of non-inferiority. It can be shown that such a test is equivalent to our testing if the malignancy rate of the additional excisions, \(\pi_\Delta\), is greater than the control THR.

$$ \text{THR}_1 = \frac{S_0\,\text{THR}_0 + \Delta S\,\pi_\Delta}{S_0 + \Delta S} \;>\; \text{THR}_0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \pi_\Delta \;>\; \text{THR}_0. $$

Both of these can also be shown to be equivalent to our previously formulated test on whether AI assistance improved physician skill, under the monotonicity assumption.

$$ \pi_\Delta = \frac{\Delta TP}{\Delta TP + \Delta FP} \;>\; \frac{TP_0}{TP_0 + FP_0} = \text{THR}_0 \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \Delta_{AI} = \frac{\Delta FP}{\Delta TP} \;<\; \frac{FP_0}{TP_0}. $$

Regardless, both approaches indicate that we should focus less on the absolute values of THR in the control and trial arms because, under randomization, the patient populations are equal. Thus, certain causal effects can be masked, especially under non-inferiority tests. Instead, we should focus on the specific number of polyps that were excised in the treatment, but not the control. This captures the causal effect of AI assistance on excisions, and using it to evaluate how these systems affect safety is much more effective.
